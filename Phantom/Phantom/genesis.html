Phantom­Genesis

How measurement gaps manufacture the appearance of structure in spectral data — and why the phantoms are properties of the hole, not the signal.

Three interactive visualizations  ·  Six theorems  ·  One open problem
01  /  The instinct

Three dots that should not exist

The paper did not begin with operator theory. It began with a picture: a rounded rectangle with three features visible and a measurement gap in the upper right corner. The features were arranged in what looked like a partial cloverleaf pattern — three lobes of what might have been a four-lobed mode, with the fourth lobe missing in the region where no data was available.

The natural interpretation was that the three visible features were real — genuine physical phenomena — and that a fourth might or might not exist under the gap. This is how measurement gaps are typically handled: the gap is a region of ignorance, and the observable data outside it is trusted.

The instinct that launched this work was the opposite: the three visible features were not real. They were artifacts created by the gap itself.

The hole in the measurement was not merely a region of missing information — it was actively creating the appearance of structure in the data that did exist. All three dots were phantoms, manufactured by the geometry and placement of the measurement gap.

Two specific aspects of this instinct were important. First, it was the hole that caused the phantoms, not whatever physical signal might exist beneath it. The gap's geometry, not the hidden field, was the source. Second, the phantom structure was not uniformly distributed. The three visible lobes were the ones nearest to the gap. The fourth — diametrically opposite — was missing. The phantom effect was distance-dependent: strongest near the hole, decaying away from it.

This was a physical instinct, not a mathematical derivation. The work that followed was an attempt to determine whether that instinct was correct and, if so, what the precise mechanism was.

02  /  Physical setting

The wave field and the gap

The motivating scenario: a bounded domain supporting superimposed wave regimes at qualitatively representative relative amplitudes (calibrated to order of magnitude, not to a specific ocean regime), with a rounded rectangular measurement gap cut out of the corner. Three regimes with distinct dispersion relations coexist — gravity swell with \(\omega^2 = gk\) at long wavelengths, wind-driven seas at intermediate scales, and capillary waves with \(\omega^2 = \sigma k^3/\rho\) at very short wavelengths where surface tension dominates and shorter waves propagate faster.

03  /  Physical analogy

The gap as a fictitious obstacle

The underlying physical intuition: a measurement gap acts like a fictitious obstacle in a wave field. When a physical wave encounters a real obstacle, the obstacle boundary enforces a condition — the wave must satisfy Dirichlet or Neumann at the boundary. The incident wave cannot satisfy this condition alone, so a scattered field is generated. That scattered field radiates outward from the obstacle boundary, decays with distance at a rate set by the Green's function, and has a spatial structure determined entirely by the obstacle geometry and the incident field's local behavior near the boundary.

A measurement gap does something analogous, but in the analysis domain rather than the physical domain. The true signal passes through the gap without knowing the gap exists — the physics is unaffected. But the analysis pipeline sets the field to zero inside the gap, imposing a fictitious Dirichlet condition that the physics never imposed. The spectral decomposition of the measured field must account for this discrepancy, and it does so by introducing phantom spectral components — the analysis-domain equivalent of a scattered field.

The phantom field is structurally analogous to a scattered response from the fictitious obstacle. It is sourced on the gap boundary \(\partial G\), with strongest amplitude near the gap and global oscillatory sidelobes persisting at distance — not a monotone decay. Its spatial structure is determined by the gap geometry and the domain's eigenbasis.

This analogy predicted three things before any mathematics was written:

(a)

The phantom features should be properties of the hole, not the signal. Different signals through the same hole should produce phantom features with the same spatial structure — same coupling matrix \(K\), same gap fingerprint — differing only in amplitude.

(b)

The phantom features should be strongest near the gap, because the scattered field from the fictitious obstacle is mediated by the reproducing kernel of the finite basis — a kernel whose main lobe is concentrated near the source point with width scaling as \(1/\sqrt{\lambda_N}\). (Note: the kernel has global sidelobes, not finite spatial range; see §6 for the precise finite-bandwidth statement.)

(c)

The phantom features should have spatial structure determined by the gap geometry — its size, shape, and placement — rather than by the true signal's spectral content.

Predictions (a) and (c) are confirmed exactly by the mathematics that follows: (a) via the coupling matrix identity $K_{mn}=\int_G\varphi_n\bar\varphi_m$ being a property of the gap alone, and (c) via the phantom's spatial structure following from the gap-dependent coupling. Prediction (b) — that phantom features should be strongest near the gap — is derived in the finite-bandwidth regime via reproducing-kernel concentration and proved in 1D (T6 on the path graph), proved with a microlocal cutoff on curved boundaries (T6c), but the full global bound on general $\partial G$ without cutoff remains open (T6, [O] in the ledger). The near-gap story is strongly motivated, qualitatively explained, and numerically illustrated; a full global theorem is still missing.

04  /  From waves to eigenmodes

The cymatics connection

The investigation shifted to eigenmode analysis on bounded domains, motivated by Chladni plate cymatics — high-order Bessel eigenmodes on a circular plate showing discrete rotational symmetry. The question: what would a missing measurement patch do to such a pattern?

The cymatics example made the eigenmode structure explicit. The true pattern had discrete rotational symmetry, and the gap broke that symmetry. Projecting the gapped field onto \(\cos(n\theta)\) for each azimuthal order \(n\) revealed that the gap created nonzero coefficients at every \(n \neq n_0\), even though the true signal had energy only at \(n_0\). These were not noise — they were coherent, deterministic, and spatially structured as valid eigenfunctions. They were phantoms.

Crucially: when the phantom modes were reconstructed spatially, they did not produce random structure. They produced a pattern that looked like the true mode — same symmetry class, same spatial wavelength, same lobe structure — but manufactured entirely by the gap.

05  /  The mathematics

Exact identities

For a single true mode \(\varphi_{n_0}\) with coefficient \(c_{n_0}\), let \(a_n = \int_G |\varphi_{n_0}|^2\) be the fraction of the mode's \(L^2\)-mass captured by the gap. The exact phantom field outside \(G\) is:

Exact spatial identity
\( f_{\mathrm{phantom}}(x) = a_n \cdot c_{n_0} \cdot \varphi_{n_0}(x) \quad \text{for } x \notin G \)

This follows from completeness and the idempotency of the mask operator \(M = I - \chi_G\). Every lobe, same amplitude, same shape.

A precision note: the raw spatial measurements outside \(G\) are the true field — \(M_G f(x) = f(x)\) for \(x \notin G\), so the data itself is not phantom. What is phantom is the spectral attribution: the modal coefficients \(\hat{c}_m\) for \(m \neq n_0\) are entirely artefactual. The spatial data is real; the modal explanation that the spectral decomposition constructs from it is fabricated by the gap geometry. The instinct that the visible features are phantom is correct at the level of their spectral interpretation — they are being attributed to modes where the true field has zero energy.

The total phantom energy satisfies an exact identity:

Energy identity
\( \sum_{m \neq n_0} |\hat{c}_m|^2 = |c_{n_0}|^2\, a_n(1 - a_n) \)

For degenerate spectrum with eigenvalue multiplicity, the identity generalizes to \( \|(I - P_\lambda)Mf\|^2 = \langle B_\lambda(I - B_\lambda)f, f\rangle \), where \(B_\lambda = P_\lambda \chi_G P_\lambda\) restricted to the eigenspace.

Three mediating kernels

Coupling kernel
\(K_{mn} = \int_G \varphi_n \bar\varphi_m\)

The gap's fingerprint in coefficient space. A property of the hole geometry alone — independent of the signal.

Reproducing kernel
\(K_N(x, x_G)\)

The response kernel. Peaks near the gap centroid \(x_G\), with main-lobe concentration and global oscillatory sidelobes. Structurally analogous to a Green-function response kernel, but not a physical Green's function: the spectral weighting is flat (equal weight per retained mode) rather than \(1/\lambda_n\).

Concentration operator
\(C_N = P_N \chi_G P_N\)

The stability kernel. Its spectrum controls whether phantom suppression is feasible and how ill-conditioned the recovery problem is.

The commutator identity \([-\Delta, M]u = -2\nabla M \cdot \nabla u - (\Delta M)u\) is supported on \(\partial G\). This is not merely a heuristic about edge effects: it has a direct physical reading. The gap boundary is a source term. The commutator is the source. The phantom field is the response.

06  /  The tension

Uniform phantoms versus distance decay

The exact identity creates a tension with the original observation. The identity says the phantom field outside \(G\) is spatially uniform — every lobe carries the same multiplicative attenuation factor $a_n$ on the true mode, regardless of distance from the gap. But the observation was that the far lobe was missing while the three near lobes were visible.

The resolution lies in the distinction between the complete-basis limit and the finite-bandwidth regime. The exact identity holds when summing all phantom mode contributions. In practice, spectral analysis retains a finite number of modes \(N\), and the finite-bandwidth phantom field is:

Finite-bandwidth phantom (small-gap regime)
\( f_{\mathrm{phantom}}^{(N)}(x) \approx -c_{n_0}|G|\varphi_{n_0}(x_G) \cdot \bigl[K_N(x, x_G) - \varphi_{n_0}(x_G)\varphi_{n_0}(x)\bigr] \)

The reproducing kernel \(K_N(x, x_G)\) concentrates its main lobe at the gap centroid with width \(\sim 1/\sqrt{\lambda_N}\), while global sidelobes persist across the domain. Near the gap, the phantom field is strong; far from the gap, the main-lobe contribution falls below detection threshold, though sidelobes remain. As \(N \to \infty\), the reproducing kernel sharpens toward \(\delta(x - x_G)\) (a Dirichlet-kernel concentration, not a broadening), and via Parseval completeness the exact spatially-uniform identity is recovered in the limit.

The original observation — three near lobes visible, far lobe missing — is therefore a finite-bandwidth effect. The total phantom energy converges to \(a_n(1-a_n)\) as \(N \to \infty\); for finite \(N\) it is a partial sum approaching that value, with spatial distribution controlled by the main-lobe width of \(K_N\).

07  /  Theorem ledger

What is proved, and what is not

The paper establishes the exact identities and the coupling-matrix structure rigorously. The distance-decay result — the original motivating observation — is qualitatively explained by the finite-bandwidth reproducing kernel argument, but not yet formally derived with a decay rate. That bridge is addressed by T6:

T6 — Boundary-layer localization (Gibbs profile)
\( s_{G,N}(z,t) = -f\!\big|_{\partial G}(z) \cdot \Gamma(\Lambda t) + O(\Lambda^{-1}), \qquad \Gamma(u) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{\mathrm{Si}(u)}{\pi} \)

where \(\Lambda = \sqrt{\lambda_N}\) is the spectral bandwidth and \(t\) is the signed distance from \(\partial G\). T6 is the quantitative version of the finite-bandwidth decay story. The leading profile \(\Gamma(\Lambda t)\) and curvature/gradient corrections are proved [K]. The \(O(\Lambda^{-1})\) error includes a bridge term \(O(\Lambda^{-1}\delta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1})\) that is proved only for the microlocal first-reflected branch; the full global bridge remains open [O] and does not affect the leading profile.
